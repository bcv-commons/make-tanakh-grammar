## Key question 1: The full inventory of categories a target profile must allow for, with Grambank/WALS/AUTOTYP ids, and which of them have no counterpart in Hebrew/Greek

### Takeaway
Grambank (195 binary/ternary features, 2,467 varieties, CC BY 4.0) plus WALS (192 feature columns, CC BY 4.0) and AUTOTYP
(v1.1.1, CC BY 4.0) cover nearly every category in the brief with a stable id; Grambank also codes Ancient Hebrew (anci1244),
Ancient Greek (anci1242) and Zinza (zinz1238), so "what the source lacks" can be read off feature by feature. The categories
with no counterpart in either source language, and therefore needing a slot the agent or team fills, are: clusivity, evidentiality,
politeness in 2nd person, switch-reference and simultaneous/sequential chaining, serial verbs, applicatives, noun class beyond
sex-based gender, numeral/possessive classifiers, paucal/trial, remoteness-graded tenses, inverse/direction marking, logophoric
pronouns, noun incorporation, and tone as grammar.

### Inferences
- Grambank and WALS are presence/absence or categorical; neither encodes the *form* of a marker or a verb template. For (B) the profile, AUTOTYP's "MaximallyInflectedVerbSynthesis" / marker-position aggregates and the Grammar Matrix "morphology" section (position classes) are the only databases that model slots; the profile needs its own template layer and can use Grambank ids as the "which values are marked" checklist.
- A per-clause universal layer (A) can be typed on UD's 27 features plus UniMorph's extra dimensions (Switch-Reference, Information Structure, Argument marking, Possession, Politeness), since both already carry Clusivity, Evident, Polite, NounClass, Deixis, irrealis, quotative and inverse values; MACULA Hebrew/Greek morphology will fill only the subset the sources mark, and the rest are explicit "to supply" slots.
- From the source-language table, the slots that are always empty in the source and frequent in the world (≥ 30 % of languages) are: clusivity (53 %), multiple past/future remoteness (31 %), indirect evidentials (30 %), serial verbs (53 %), applicatives (33 %), causative affix (73 %), clause chaining/simultaneity (29–34 %), particles for mood/aspect (38–40 %). Rare but hard ones: switch-reference (17 %), direct evidentials (15 %), politeness (14 %), inverse (5 %), logophoric (7 %), trial/paucal (≤ 1 %).

### Gaps
- No Grambank or WALS id exists for mirativity, obviation, conditionals as a construction, tail-head linkage, or information-structure marking; only UD (Mood=Adm) and the Grammar Matrix (info-str) name them.
- The Grambank codings for Ancient Hebrew/Greek were not checked against the coders' comments; a few values (GB137 = 1, GB059 = 1, GB086 = 0 for Hebrew; GB167 = 1 for Greek) look doubtful.
- Kuni-Boazi is not in Grambank, WALS or (as far as fetched) AUTOTYP, so the Papuan pilot's profile has no database baseline.

## Key question 2: How the Papuan and Bantu pilots differ from the Hebrew/Greek clause model, and what translators did

### Takeaway
Kuni-Boazi is an Anim (Fly River) language, not Trans-New Guinea, of about 4,500 speakers; its area's typology (clause chains of medial verbs with switch-reference and sequence/simultaneity, tail-head linkage, quotative constructions) is documented for neighbouring families (Awyu-Ndumut, Finisterre, Amele), and the translation literature records that translated Papuan texts show fewer topic markers, tail-head linkages and shorter chains than native texts. Zinza (Bantu JE.23) has the typical Bantu profile per Grambank (remoteness tenses, applicative, causative, affixal negation, no sex-based gender) and a phrasal tone system described by Odden (2000); Bantu-specific translation problems in the literature are noun-class assignment of theological terms (Doke 1966) and applicative/derivational verb forms.

### Cited Findings

*Kuni-Boazi and Papuan clause structure*
- Kuni-Boazi: Glottolog tree Anim → Marind-Boazi-Yaqai → Boazi → Kuni-Boazi; Boazi languages (Lake Murray) were "previously classified in the Marind branch"; the Anim family has "four subfamilies"; "about 4,500 native speakers as of 2007"; Glottolog code discrepancy kuni1265 (language) vs kuni1266 (dialect "Kuni (Western Province)") — [Glottolog bian1251](https://glottolog.org/resource/languoid/id/bian1251), [Boazi language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boazi_language), [Anim languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anim_languages), [Boazi languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boazi_languages)
- Clause chaining: "a clause chain is considered to comprise one or more 'medial' clauses, followed by a single 'final' clause"; chains run "one to twenty or more medial clauses" with the final verb carrying tense, mood and subject; found in "Papua New Guinea, the Amazon, and the Caucasus" — [Editorial: Acquisition of Clause Chaining](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7661487/), [Nungon acquisition](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7358287/)
- Medial verbs (Awyu-Ndumut, Korowai): "medial verbs can't carry the full range of tense, mood, person, and number distinctions. Instead, they encode switch reference and temporal relations between adjacent clauses … slots for switch reference (same or different subject in the next clause) and temporality (sequence versus simultaneity)" — [VU Awyu-Ndumut: Mandobo](https://www2.fgw.vu.nl/awyu-ndumut/mandobo.php)
- Finisterre languages: "same-subject suffixes do not also involve agreement with the marked clause's subject, while different-subject suffixes do"; Foley & Olson (1985) and Van Valin & LaPolla (1997) are the standard treatments of the dependency-plus-coordination pattern — [Clause chaining in Finisterre Papuan languages](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/clause-chaining-in-finisterre-papuan-languages/), [Clause Chaining in the Languages of the World (OUP 2024)](https://academic.oup.com/book/59261/chapter/499379047)
- Foley's analysis: clause chains are "a type of coordination, distinguished by the type of constituent coordinated, S versus IP" — [Foley, Clause linkage and Nexus in Papuan languages](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/slcs.121.02fol/html)
- Roberts 1997, "Switch-reference in Papua New Guinea: a preliminary survey", Pacific Linguistics A-87, pp. 101–241: PNG "is where switch-reference occurs in more adjacent languages than anywhere else in the world"; the ANU re-release is CC BY-SA 4.0 (DOI 10.15144/PL-A87.101) — [ANU repository](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstreams/d5fb40af-522e-4bc0-b859-c9d02342302c/download); Roberts 1988 "Amele switch-reference and the Theory of Grammar", *Linguistic Inquiry* — [Glottolog ref](https://glottolog.org/resource/reference/id/157704)

- Translated vs native Papuan texts: a *Journal of Translation* 1(2) 2005 article (Dooley, Mbyá Guaraní case study) argues "Bible translations often mix target-language patterns at the micro level with source-text patterns at the macro level"; per the search-engine summary of the PDF (full text could not be downloaded: the sil.org URL returned HTML), it also notes that "shorter clause chains and reduced citation margins seem to relate specifically to translation" and that Bible translations in Irian Jaya "showed relatively few topic markers and tail-head linkages, and shorter clause chains" — [SIL Brazil record](https://www.silbrazil.org/resources/archives/40256), [JoT 1(2) PDF](https://www.sil.org/system/files/reapdata/16/36/49/163649157157074771562069031938308553559/siljot2005_2_01.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Kuni the profile must carry chain structure (medial vs final verb, SS/DS, sequence/simultaneity) as clause-level relations, not verb features: the Hebrew wayyiqtol chain and Greek participle chains give sequence but never subject-switch, so the source clause layer must expose "same/different subject as next clause" computed from MACULA's participant references, and leave the choice of a THL recap to a discourse rule.
- For Zinza the profile needs (i) a noun-class assignment per lexeme with concord targets (GB170–172, GB198), (ii) a tense slot with remoteness (GB309) that Hebrew never marks, (iii) valency extensions (applicative GB103/104, causative GB155) that Greek/Hebrew express by prepositions or Hiphil, and (iv) tone as a tense/negation exponent, which "parts" segmentation must treat as a separate tier.

### Gaps
- No grammar of Kuni-Boazi was found online; the family-level typology is inferred from neighbouring Papuan families.
- De Vries 1992 (BT) and Dooley 2005 (JoT) full texts were not retrieved; the Irian Jaya figures are second-hand.
- No Bible-translation article specifically on Bantu applicatives or tone-marked tense was found in *The Bible Translator* / *Journal of Translation* searches.

## Key question 3: What the Bible-translation literature says about rendering categories Hebrew/Greek lack (evidentials, clusivity, switch-reference, serial verbs, tail-head linkage, noun classes, honorifics)

### Takeaway
SIL's accumulated verse-level guidance exists for clusivity (Pickett & Cowan 1962 and the SIL Translation Department 1999, now in tips.translation.bible) and for Japanese/Korean honorifics (Doi 2022; a Korean Mark 14 study), while evidentials, switch-reference and serial verbs are documented mainly in field grammars and discourse manuals (Dooley & Levinsohn; Levinsohn; Nicolle), not in dedicated journal articles; the one empirical study of evidentials in a Bible (Ayacucho Quechua) found them absent or over-used.

### Cited Findings
- Clusivity, verse by verse: Semitic and Indo-European "do not make that distinction, so translators of languages that do have to choose a form every time 'we,' 'our,' or 'us' appears"; 2 Cor 5:1 → exclusive (Mal, Karbi and Copainalá Zoque chose inclusive); Phil 4:20 → inclusive; 2 Pet 1:16 → exclusive for most "we" but inclusive for "our Lord"; Acts 13:17 disputed: Pickett & Cowan (1962, *Notes on Translation*) inclusive vs SIL International Translation Department (1999) exclusive — [2 Cor 5:1](https://tips.translation.bible/story/inclusive-vs-exclusive-pronoun-2cor-51/), [Phil 4:20](https://tips.translation.bible/?p=1676), [2 Pet 1:16](https://tips.translation.bible/story/inclusive-vs-exclusive-pronoun-2pet-116/), [Acts 13:17](https://tips.translation.bible/story/inclusive-vs-exclusive-pronoun-acts-1317/)
- Honorifics, Japanese: Japanese has "levels of politeness … ranging from addressing someone with contempt to the highest level of reference, used for God"; the 2017 Shinkaiyaku uses honorific prefixes (o-hanashi "speak", o-tate "set up", o-yorokobi, o-meiji "commandment"), the divine prefix mi- (mi-sumai), respectful benefactives (kudasaru) and kudasai for polite imperatives; source: S. E. Doi, *Journal of Translation* 18 (2022), p. 37ff — [1 Chr 17:23](https://tips.translation.bible/tip_verse/1chr-1723/), [Ps 13:1](https://tips.translation.bible/story/hide-japanese-honorifics/), [Ps 40:6](https://tips.translation.bible/story/japanese-honorifics-psalm-406/), [2 Chr 2:16](https://tips.translation.bible/story/japanese-honorifics-2-chronicles-216/), [John 12:49](https://tips.translation.bible/story/japanese-honorifics-john-1249/)
- Honorifics, Korean (Mark 14 study): "Korean addressee honorifics, viz. the honorific second person pronouns and the honorific verb ending forms, are the most complex systems among the honorific languages"; the source text "has no grammatical marker for the translator to follow" — [Kyobo scholar record](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010023749839)
- Evidentials in a published Bible: Bernárdez Sanchís 2020 (*Archivum* 70(2): 27–58) on Ayacucho Quechua Bible and a Cusco Quechua Book of Mormon: "complete absence of evidential markers or overabundance of the direct evidence marker in absence of any other" — [hispadoc PDF](https://hispadoc.es/descarga/articulo/7835112.pdf); background: Floyd 1999 *The Structure of Evidential Categories in Wanka Quechua* (SIL) — [review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/19413/pdf); Tuyuca has "a mandatory five-term evidential system" and a Wycliffe NT (2011) — [DBS Tuyuca NT](https://dbs.org/bibles/TUEWBT); Barnes 1984 "Evidentials in the Tuyuca verb", *IJAL* — [WALS refdb](https://wals.info/refdb/record/Barnes-1984)
- Serial verbs in translation: Gbaya renders the collapsing house (two builders parable) "by a serial verb construction 'break-put ground'"; Saliba "makes extensive use of serial verb constructions" (SIL paper on clause linking) — [tips.translation.bible BT source](https://tips.translation.bible/tip_source/the-bible-translator/), [Saliba clause linking, SIL](https://www.sil.org/system/files/reapdata/84/51/99/84519975332623823018233958599402770854/Semantics_Clause_Linking_Saliba_Mar2019.pdf)
- Word-class mismatch as a general principle: Nida 1959, "what is a noun in Greek must be rendered as a verb in other languages" — [Nida 1959 PDF](https://translation.bible/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/nida-1959-principles-of-translation-as-exemplified-by-bible-translating.pdf)
- SIL discourse manuals (the training route for switch-reference, THL, reported speech, participant reference): Dooley & Levinsohn, *Analyzing Discourse: A Manual of Basic Concepts*, SIL 2001 (172 pp., ISBN 1556711158), revised edition 2023 (ISBN 978-1556714900, 178 pp.) — [LINGUIST List 13.1747](https://linguistlist.org/issues/13/1747/), [LINGUIST List 34.1847](https://dev.linguistlist.org/issues/34/1847/); Levinsohn, *Self-Instruction Materials on Narrative Discourse Analysis* (SIL 2023, 192 pp.): "ordering of constituents within sentences, backgrounding and highlighting devices, connectives, reporting conversation in narrative, and participant reference" — [koine-greek.com](https://koine-greek.com/2023/01/11/levinsohns-discourse-analysis-materials/); Levinsohn, Nicolle & Stirtz, *Discourse Analysis and Bible Translation* (SIL) with sections "addressed specifically to speakers of verb-initial, verb-medial, and verb-final languages" — [Blackwell's](https://blackwells.co.uk/bookshop/product/Discourse-Analysis-and-Bible-Translation-by-Stephen-H-Levinsohn-Steve-Nicolle-Tim-Stirtz/9781556715518)
- *The Bible Translator* (since 1950) devoted its 2013 section meeting to "Valency in Bible Translation" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bible_Translator); *Notes on Translation* issues from 1997/1999 list "relative clauses in Cameroonian languages and word-order issues in OV languages" — [NOT 11(3)](https://www.silcam.org/resources/archives/88601), [NOT 13(4)](https://www.silcam.org/resources/archives/90067)
- Larson, *Meaning-Based Translation*: 1st ed. 1984 (x, 537 pp.), 2nd ed. 1998 (x, 586 pp., University Press of America) — [catalogue record](https://lib.pnu.ac.ir/inventory/4/43373.htm)

### Inferences
- The clusivity decisions are verse-specific and sometimes disputed between SIL sources, so a per-clause "we" slot must allow a value, a confidence and a note, not a deterministic rule; the same holds for honorific level, which depends on speaker–addressee–referent triads that MACULA's speaker layer and bcv-query's speaker layer (already in `orig/heb.sqlite`) partly supply.
- The Quechua finding (markers absent or over-used) is the failure mode to test for in any evidential language: a drafting rule that defaults to one marker will reproduce it.

### Gaps
- No *Journal of Translation* or *Notes on Translation* article on evidentials, switch-reference or serial verbs was found by search; the SIL archive is only partly indexed by web search.
- Larson's chapter list (person, tense/aspect, discourse chapters) could not be retrieved from any catalogue; Thai honorific literature was not found.

## Key question 4: Precedents for a formal, machine-readable target-language profile (Grammar Matrix choices file, AGGREGATION, FLEx, PAWS, Grambank/WALS as profiles)

### Takeaway
The LinGO Grammar Matrix "choices file" is the closest precedent: a flat `section=…` / `key=value` text file produced by a 26-section typological questionnaire (word order, number, person, gender, case, adnominal possession, direct-inverse, TAM, evidentials, negation, coordination, yes/no and wh-questions, information structure, argument optionality, nominalized clauses, light verbs, clausal complements and modifiers, lexicon, morphology with position classes), MIT-licensed, used for "more than 40 languages" in teaching by 2010 and as the target of AGGREGATION's inference from IGT (27 development + 5 held-out languages in 2022); FLEx (LGPL 2.1+) models morphology as position-class templates with slots; Grambank itself is a usable presence/absence profile but not a realization model.

### Cited Findings
- Grammar Matrix licence: "copyright (c) 2002- Emily M. Bender and licensed under the MIT license" — [LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/delph-in/matrix/master/LICENSE); repository [github.com/delph-in/matrix](https://github.com/delph-in/matrix) ("a DELPH-IN project for creating ('customizing') HPSG grammars"; used "to teach university courses in grammar engineering" and to jumpstart Zhong (Chinese) and INDRA (Indonesian))
- Questionnaire sections (from `web/matrixdef`, fetched 2026-10-22): general, word-order, number, person, gender, case, adnom-poss "Adnominal Possession", direct-inverse, tense-aspect-mood, evidentials, other-features, sentential-negation, coordination, matrix-yes-no, wh-q "Constituent (wh-) Questions", info-str "Information Structure", arg-opt "Argument Optionality", nominalclause, lvc "Light Verb Constructions", clausal-comp, clausalmods "Clausal Modifiers", lexicon, morphology, toolbox-import, test-sentences, gen-options, ToolboxLexicon — [matrixdef](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/delph-in/matrix/master/web/matrixdef)
- Choices-file format (sample Japanese file): `version=20`, `section=word-order`, `word-order=v-final`, `has-dets=yes`, `has-aux=yes`, `aux-comp-order=after`; `section=person`, `person=1-2-3`; `section=case`, `case-marking=nom-acc`; `section=tense-aspect`, `past=on`, `nonpast=on`, `aspect1_name=prog`; `section=sentential-negation`, `infl-neg=on`; `section=matrix-yes-no`, `q-part-order=after`, `q-part-orth=か`; `section=arg-opt`, `subj-drop=subj-drop-all`, `obj-drop=obj-drop-all`; `section=lexicon`, `noun1_name=common`, `noun1_feat1_name=person` — [sample-choices/japanese](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/delph-in/matrix/master/web/sample-choices/japanese); sample files available: empty, japanese, maltese, mini-english, minimal, sahaptin (GitHub API listing of `web/sample-choices`, 2026-10-22)

- AGGREGATION: aims to "automate the construction of grammar fragments" from IGT on top of the Grammar Matrix; NSF grants BCS-1160274 and BCS-1561833; software: inference pipeline, Morphotactic Visualization, Xigt ("XML format for Interlinear Glossed Text"), xigtviz; no licence stated on the page — [AGGREGATION project](https://depts.washington.edu/uwcl/aggregation/)

- Grambank as a profile: CC BY 4.0; per-language rows (e.g. the Zinza row in KQ1) are a binary checklist of what is marked, with "?" for uncoded; Grambank's parameters table carries meta-columns Boundness, Flexivity, Gender_or_Noun_Class, Locus_of_Marking, Word_Order, Informativity — `download/grambank/v1.0.3/parameters.csv`; [grambank GitHub](https://github.com/grambank/grambank)
- UD and UniMorph as the feature vocabulary (KQ1): UD 27 features; UniMorph 23 dimensions / 212 features with LGSPEC extensions — [UD feat](https://universaldependencies.org/u/feat/index.html), [P15-2111](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P15-2111.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Matrix questionnaire can serve as the profile's backbone for syntax and inflection: its sections map one-to-one onto most of the inventory in KQ1 (evidentials, direct-inverse, info-str, arg-opt, clausal-comp, adnom-poss), its format is trivially machine-readable, and the licence (MIT) permits reuse. What it lacks is exactly what the pilots need most: clause chaining/switch-reference, serial verbs, applicatives/causatives as a library, noun-class concord beyond "gender", tone, honorific levels, and any discourse-level device (tail-head linkage, quotative frames); those sections would have to be added in the same `section=/key=value` style.
- AGGREGATION's lesson for (C) is that inferred choices are usable for parsing coverage but "too noisy" as a finished profile; the same will hold for Grambank values derived from alignment (this repo's recipes), which should be stored with the evidence and a confidence, as the recipes already do with lift thresholds.

### Gaps
- The Matrix wiki's current library list (evidentials, information structure, possession, clausal complements, wh-questions) was only confirmed through `matrixdef`, since the customization server and the wiki's up-to-date library page were unreachable or sparse; dates of each library were not retrieved.
- The exact number of languages with Matrix-derived grammars after 2010 was not found (only "more than 40" as of 2010 and the ten named grammars).
- AGGREGATION's per-language accuracy for word order/case inference was not retrieved (abstracts only).

## Key question 5: Which categories parallel-Bible typology recovered reliably, and which not

### Takeaway
Word order (object/verb, adposition/noun, numeral/noun, relative/noun) is recovered from Bible alignments at 85–96 % agreement with WALS (Östling 2015; Östling & Kurfalı 2023); affixation type reaches about 85 % but prefixing is weak (recall 74 %, precision 65 %), and the method "is not able to fully separate inflectional morphology from derivational morphology, or affixes from clitics"; tense was recovered as marker pivots for 1,163 languages without a per-language accuracy against WALS (Asgari & Schütze 2017); language embeddings mostly do not encode typology (Östling & Kurfalı 2023).

### Cited Findings
- Corpus: Mayer & Cysouw 2014 (LREC) "over 900 translations in more than 830 language varieties", "tokenized … and Unicode normalized"; "Mainly due to copyright restrictions only portions of the texts are made publicly available" — [L14-1215](https://aclanthology.org/L14-1215/); the 2014 corpus "is no longer available"; the JHU Bible Corpus (2020) has "over 4000 translations" — [LREC 2020](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2020.lrec-1.352.pdf)

### Inferences
- Realization rules learned from alignment (C) will be reliable where the signal is positional and frequent (constituent order, adposition side, numeral/relative order: ≥ 90 %), middling for affix side (≈ 70–85 %, prefixes worst), and only candidate-generating for TAM markers (Asgari's pivots need a human label); nothing in the literature recovers evidentials, clusivity, switch-reference or honorific levels automatically, so these must come from the store, the team, or the agent's reading of the human text.
- Genre bias is real (adjective order in French NTs): rules learned on a Bible corpus describe the translation's register, which for a drafting tool is the right register but must not be presented as the language's typology.

### Gaps
- No per-language precision for tense/aspect categories against WALS 66A–68A exists in the computational literature found; Dahl's parallel-text TAME work is on European languages and perfects.
- Mayer & Cysouw's corpus is no longer distributed; the JHU corpus's licence status was not checked.

## Key question 6: How polysynthetic and isolating languages challenge a "parts" (prefix + stem + suffix) word representation

### Takeaway
At the two ends of WALS 22A (0–1 vs 12–13 inflectional categories per verb word) and 20A (exclusively isolating vs exclusively tonal/ablaut), a linear prefix–stem–suffix split either has nothing to segment (isolating: tense/aspect/mood carried by free particles, GB519–521, 24–40 % of languages) or far too much (polysynthetic: many derivational plus inflectional morphemes with boundary phonology; segmentation is the bottleneck in Inuktitut MT), and tone (WALS 69A "tense-aspect tone"; Bantu) and ablaut are exponents with no linear position at all.

### Cited Findings
- WALS 22A (my count, 145 languages): 0–1 category per word 5, 2–3: 24, 4–5: 52, 6–7: 31, 8–9: 24, 10–11: 7, 12–13: 2 — [WALS 22A](https://wals.info/feature/22A); 20A (165): exclusively concatenative 125, exclusively isolating 16, isolating/concatenative 13, ablaut/concatenative 5, exclusively tonal 3, tonal/concatenative 2 — [WALS 20A](https://wals.info/feature/20A); 26A (969): "Little affixation" 141 — [WALS 26A](https://wals.info/feature/26A); 69A: "No tense-aspect inflection" 152 and "Tense-aspect tone" 13 of 1,131 — [WALS 69A](https://wals.info/feature/69A)
- Grambank: mood/aspect/tense marked by a non-inflecting particle GB519 40 %, GB520 38 %, GB521 24 %; noun incorporation GB124 16 %; verb compounding GB122; classificatory verbs GB116; "a notably small number, i.e. about 100 or less, of verb roots" GB129 — `download/grambank/v1.0.3/parameters.csv`; counts from [grambank values.csv](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/grambank/grambank/master/cldf/values.csv)
- Östling & Kurfalı 2023: automatic affixation typing "is not able to fully separate inflectional morphology from derivational morphology, or affixes from clitics"; Hammarström 2021 "confirmed that automatic estimation of affixation type is quite challenging" — [2023.cl-4.6](https://aclanthology.org/2023.cl-4.6.pdf)
- Inuit–Yupik: "pervasive use of derivational morphemes in addition to rich sets of inflectional suffixes and phonological challenges at morpheme boundaries" — [TAL 2021 paper](https://aclweb.org/anthology/2021.tal-3.3.pdf); Inuktitut MT thesis: "segmentation is a key preprocessing step, and … existing segmenters were not designed with polysynthetic languages in mind" — [Roest 2020](https://fse.studenttheses.ub.rug.nl/23334/1/mAI_2020_Christian_Roest.pdf); WMT 2020 English–Inuktitut: morphological segmentation, Greenlandic data and contextual embeddings each "showed some promise, the results are mixed" — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10285558); "Neural polysynthetic language modelling": "stemming, lemmatization, or subword modelling may not suffice" — [Schwartz et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05477)
- AUTOTYP models verb structure by marker position ("MaximallyInflectedVerbInflectionCategoriesAggregatedByMarkerPosition", "…HasMultipleExponents") rather than by a fixed prefix/suffix pair, and has a "Flexivity" (allomorphy) and "PhonologicalFusion" definition set — [autotyp datasets](https://github.com/autotyp/autotyp-data/blob/master/datasets.md)
- Bantu: eastern languages stack derivational suffixes "sometimes exuberantly"; Northwest Bantu has "a high number of floating tones" and few "tonally underspecified morphemes" — [Bantu Languages introduction](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-02504383v1/file/Introduction_The_Bantu_Languages_prefinal.pdf)
- FLEx handles this with position-class templates per word class (prefix and suffix slots, obligatory/optional) and allomorph environments — [ComputEL 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.computel-1.8.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "parts" view must become (i) an ordered list of position classes with zero or many fillers (not one prefix and one suffix), (ii) a separate tone/ablaut tier that can realise a category without a segment (Zinza tense/negation, Kuni tone marks), and (iii) a clause-level fallback where the category is realised by a free particle or word order rather than a morph (isolating languages). AUTOTYP's marker-position model and FLEx templates are the two existing data models to copy.
- For polysynthetic targets, learned realization rules will have to operate on morph sequences from a dedicated analyser (as the Inuktitut MT work does) rather than on whitespace tokens; the store's "confirmed affixes" step already in this repo is that analyser's seed.

### Gaps
- No Bible-translation-specific study of polysynthetic or isolating targets was found (searches returned only MT papers); no figure for how many Bible-target languages fall at each end of WALS 22A.
