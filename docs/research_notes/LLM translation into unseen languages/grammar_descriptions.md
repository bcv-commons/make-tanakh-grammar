# Linguistic descriptions as LLM translation aids for unseen languages (2023-2026)

Scope: what kind of grammatical knowledge (grammar prose, grammar sketches, interlinear glosses, paradigms, dictionaries, parallel sentences) measurably improves LLM translation into or out of a language the model cannot write, in what form, and what does not. Numbers are given with model, language, data size and direction. Research date: 2026-10-22. Aimed at our setting: Kuni (Papuan) and Zinza (Bantu), ~8,000-verse NT parallel corpus, prompt material ~1 MB, chrF at the floor into the language, 41-44% of the human translation's verb forms absent from the corpus.

A note on names: the Zhuang paper the brief calls "Guo et al." is by Chen Zhang, Xiao Liu, Jiuheng Lin and Yansong Feng (Peking University); the Manchu paper is Pei et al.; Kalamang (MTOB) is itself a Papuan language (West Bomberai), so the Kalamang literature is the only direct Papuan evidence found.

## Key question 1: What helped, by how much, in which direction (MTOB, Aycock, LingoLLM, DiPMT++, GrammaMT, Court & Elsner, 2025-2026 follow-ups)

### Takeaway
Across every study that ablated the material, parallel sentences and a dictionary carry nearly the whole gain; grammar prose adds 0-2 chrF on top of them, sometimes negative, and is reliably below parallel-only when used alone. Into-the-language scores are consistently lower and the human-judged fluency of into-language output is poor even when chrF looks respectable; the one study that measured it found native speakers preferred the LLM's Kanuri fluency only 7-15% of the time.

### Cited Findings

**MTOB (Tanzer et al., ICLR 2024; Kalamang, <200 speakers)**
- Materials: a 573-page grammar (~217,388 tokens as plaintext, GPT-3 tokenizer; ~518,884 tokens in LaTeX); a medium chunk Gm of ~50K tokens (overview chapter, morpheme table, full word list); a long chunk Gl of ~100K tokens (chapters judged most important); 500 parallel sentences after removing grammar examples (400 train / 100 test; at most ~2,000 including grammar examples); a word list of 2,531 Kalamang entries; test set 100 sentences, 50 per direction. — [MTOB html](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16575v2)
- Best baseline: Claude 2 with W+S+Gl: 44.7 chrF kgv→eng, 45.8 chrF eng→kgv; the human learner (first author, ~10 hours with the grammar) 51.6 / 57.0. Without context, models "essentially cannot translate". — [MTOB abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16575); [MTOB html](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16575v2)
- Among retrieved context types, sentences (S) help most, then word-list entries (W), then grammar excerpts (Gs/Ge). The authors say retrieved grammar excerpts "are lexically similar but often lack useful grammar explanations, so they act like extra sentences with glosses", and attribute that to retrieval failure rather than the models' inability to use grammar. The 100K-token Gl chunk gave what they call a "qualitative leap" in eng→kgv output (more grammatical Kalamang), though inconsistent. Longest-common-substring retrieval beat embedding retrieval. Fine-tuning on the grammar text harmed results. — [MTOB html](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16575v2)
- Gemini 1.5 technical report, Table 55 (MTOB): Gemini 1.5 Pro with the full book: human rating 5.46/6 (chrF 59.0, BLEURT 73.0); half book 5.38 (chrF 59.1, BLEURT 74.4); human learner 5.60 (chrF 57.0, BLEURT 72.0); Gemini 1.5 Flash full book 4.66 (chrF 52.0). The search summary labelled these kgv→eng, but the human chrF 57.0 is MTOB's eng→kgv figure, and the Kanuri paper below quotes "59.00 for Gemini 1.5 Pro" for its English→X replication, so these are most likely eng→kgv numbers. A "no book 55.5 chrF" row quoted in the same search summary could not be verified and looks implausible against every other zero-shot Kalamang result (11-17 chrF); treat it as unconfirmed. — [Gemini 1.5 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05530); [Shaw et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06665)

**Aycock, Stap, Wu, Monz, Sima'an (ICLR 2025), "Can LLMs really learn to translate a low-resource language from one grammar book?"**
- Model Gemini-1.5-Flash-001 (plus Llama-3.1 instruct and LoRA-fine-tuned); metric chrF++; Kalamang test set 100; book split into its parallel part (Book-para: 4,489 lines, 17,858 tokens) and its explanations (Book-non-para: 2,282 lines, 81,268 tokens). — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- Gemini chrF++ (eng→kgv / kgv→eng, prompt tokens): 0-shot 11.0 / 12.7; wordlist W 29.1 / 27.9 (9.0k); Book-para (the book's examples only) 30.8 / 34.7 (18.3k); Book-non-para (explanations only) 22.6 / 27.5 (81.3k); Book-all 34.4 / 34.4 (99.6k); Book-all + W 38.3 / 39.6; Book-all + W + 400 extra training pairs 43.7 / 46.1 (113.4k); Parabook + Paratrain 40.7 / 46.6 (29.4k); 5*-shot retrieved examples alone 38.9 / 33.4 (0.8k tokens). — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- A typological-feature prompt (Grambank/WALS-style features, 68.4k tokens) gave 10.8 / 13.9, i.e. nothing over 0-shot; Typ + Book-para 31.4 / 35.2 vs Book-para 30.8 / 34.7. — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- Fine-tuned NLLB-1.3B on the same parallel data: Parabook 34.2 eng→kgv / 28.6 kgv→eng vs Gemini in-context 26.6 / 33.1; Parabook + Paratrain 38.7 / 36.9 vs Gemini 33.4 / 38.5 (Table 4; the Gemini figures there differ from Table 2's, presumably a different subset). — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- Seen low-resource languages: for Guarani the explanations-only prompt lowered Gemini below 0-shot (eng→gug 19.3 vs 26.6; gug→eng 34.5 vs 41.3); Nepali was flat (42.5 → 41.8). — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- Performance tracks lexical coverage, not prompt size: chrF++ vs the share of test word types covered by the prompt, r = 0.92 (eng→kgv) and 0.93 (kgv→eng), R² 0.84 / 0.87; chrF++ vs prompt tokens not significant (p = 0.997 / 0.78). Book-all covers 77.0% of test types with 99.6k tokens; Book-para 69.4% with 18.3k; Book-non-para 66.3% with 81.3k; wordlist 58.5% with 9.0k. — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- Authors' explanation: LLMs learn translation "more effectively from unannotated parallel examples than from explained grammar"; parallel data resembles instruction-tuning data; relevant content gets harder to retrieve as context grows (citing Hsieh et al. 2024, RULER), and Book-para has a higher ratio of relevant to irrelevant content than Book-all; the full book costs ~5× the tokens of Book-para for similar performance. Conclusion: "no evidence that long-context LLMs can make effective use of grammatical explanations for XLR translation"; collect parallel data rather than descriptions. — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2); [ICLR page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/29173)

**LingoLLM / "Hire a Linguist!" (K. Zhang et al., Findings of ACL 2024)**
- Pipeline: finite-state morphological analyzer (foma) splits words into stems and features; dictionary lookup with edit-distance fallback and cross-entry links; the grammar book goes into the prompt whole if it fits, else GPT-4 summarizes it (books can exceed 50,000 tokens); the LLM is told to annotate features before translating. 8 languages (Manchu, Gitksan, Uspanteko, Natugu, Tsez, Wolof, Arapaho, Bribri), 10 directions. — [LingoLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.18025v2)
- GPT-4 spBLEU averaged over the 10 directions: zero-shot 0.5; few-shot 3.2; dictionary-only LingoLLM 8.2; full LingoLLM (dictionary + grammar + morphological analysis) 10.5. Mixtral: zero-shot 1.3, LingoLLM 5.9. So the grammar book plus morphological analysis added ~2.3 spBLEU over the dictionary alone. — [LingoLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.18025v2); [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18025v2)
- Morphological analysis on Gitksan raised BLEURT from 0.4573 to 0.5448 (+19%). For Arapaho, adding the grammar's morphology chapters gave no significant translation gain "because the morphological analyzer already supplies stems and features". Dictionary-only output "often lacked coherence or had the wrong sentence type" (qualitative). Bribri gained least, attributed to low dictionary coverage. Human vs model on 35 Manchu sentences: few-shot 0.82 BLEU, LingoLLM 9.12, human 20.32. Works only on Romanized scripts. — [LingoLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.18025v2)
- The per-direction mapping (which of the 10 directions are into the endangered language) could not be recovered from the extracted table; see Gaps.

**DiPMT++ / "Teaching LLMs an unseen language on the fly" (C. Zhang et al., Findings of ACL 2024; Zhuang)**
- Resources: Zhuang-Chinese dictionary 16,031 words, Chinese-Zhuang 13,618; 4,944 parallel pairs (government reports, textbooks, dictionary examples); test 200 pairs; 3-shot exemplars retrieved by BM25, dictionary entries with fuzzy matching, bilingual lexicon induction (BLI) and synonym expansion. — [DiPMT++](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.19167v2)
- GPT-4 direct: 0.0 BLEU / 7.0 chrF zh→za, 1.4 / 3.9 za→zh; GPT-4 DiPMT++: 15.7 / 46.1 zh→za, 31.9 / 29.1 za→zh. Qwen-72B: DiPMT 5.1 / 31.7 → DiPMT++ 16.4 / 45.1 (zh→za). Fine-tuned Llama-2-7B on the same data: 6.5 / 33.2 zh→za. — [DiPMT++](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.19167v2)
- Ablations (Qwen-14B, zh→za BLEU): full 12.6; without fuzzy dictionary matching 9.1; without BLI 10.1; without synonym expansion 11.7; random exemplars instead of BM25 9.2; POS-sequence exemplars 7.8. Dictionary coverage of test Zhuang words 67% → 79% after BLI. — [DiPMT++](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.19167v2)
- Adding monolingual Zhuang text to the prompt did not help (GPT-3.5 zh→za BLEU: 13.3 baseline; +1K tokens 14.2; +2K 13.6; +5K 13.1); Qwen-14B produced no meaningful output with +1K tokens; GPT-3.5 at +5K "fabricated words not in the prompt". — [DiPMT++](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.19167v2)
- Explicit syntax pilot (10 zh→za samples, GPT-3.5): stating the modifier-order rule directly, 1/10 correct; chain-of-thought with hints identifying the modifier and the modified element, 7/10. Authors: understanding grammar rules "is a complex procedure and still presents significant challenges". — [DiPMT++](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.19167v2)
- Kalamang replication (dictionary and corpus ~10% of Zhuang's; 50 test instances per direction): GPT-3.5 MTOB W+S baseline 0.0 BLEU / 30.6 chrF eng→kgv, 6.6 / 31.1 kgv→eng; DiPMT++ 2.9 / 35.5 and 11.4 / 37.0. — [DiPMT++](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.19167v2)

**GrammaMT (Ramos et al., ACL 2025; interlinear-gloss prompting)**
- All reported tables are from the low-resource language into English ("Due to the limited availability of IGT datasets, we focus on translating into English"); the reverse direction appears only as a plot (Figure 6) with no numbers. — [GrammaMT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18702v2); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1447)
- SIGMORPHON 2023 languages (Gitksan, Lezgi, Natugu, Tsez), Llama-3 70B, avg BLEU / chrF++: few-shot 3.94 / 21.85; gloss-shot (examples with glosses) 3.41 / 22.50; chain-gloss (model writes its own gloss first) 4.25 / 20.84; model-gloss (gloss from a trained glosser, GlossLM) 15.97 / 41.45. GPT-4o: 4.88 / 22.71 few-shot vs 18.69 / 43.66 model-gloss. — [GrammaMT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18702v2)
- Gloss accuracy decides it: Llama-3 70B's own glosses reach 8.17% morpheme accuracy (chrF++ 16.53) vs GlossLM 51.16% (66.02). Oracle gold glosses give +17.46 BLEU (±6.6) over few-shot. Removing grammatical labels from glosses lowers performance, so the gain is not just lemma-level word-by-word translation. — [GrammaMT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18702v2)
- Bantu data point: Swahili→English (GlossLM corpus, Llama-3 70B) BLEU few-shot 22.41, gloss-shot 22.18, chain-gloss 23.53; Yoruba 11.98 → 16.32 (gloss-shot). — [GrammaMT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18702v2)

**Court & Elsner (WMT 2024), Southern Quechua→Spanish**
- Metric BLEURT; context from a digitized dictionary, grammar lessons (1-3 sentences plus tables/examples each) and the three best LCS-matched corpus sentences. GPT-3.5: baseline 0.19 → morph (morpheme-level dictionary glosses) 0.44, grammar 0.23, corpus 0.27, all three 0.43. GPT-4o: baseline 0.66 → corpus 0.59, grammar 0.56, morph 0.54, all 0.57 (every context hurt). Gemini 1.5 Pro: 0.56 → morph 0.61. Llama 3 8B: 0.15 → morph 0.39. Manual (human-cleaned) retrieval raised morph to 0.56 / 0.63 / 0.66 / 0.49. — [Court & Elsner](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.15625v3); [WMT page](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wmt-1.125)
- Error counts per 50 sentences, GPT-3.5: mistranslations 45 (baseline) → 11 (morph), but target-fluency errors 0 → 14 and grammatical divergences (subject + TAM) 0 → 24; TAM divergences far outnumbered subject ones. Authors: grammar explanations "do little to help the newest generation of LLMs" even when relevant and exemplified; "it is not clear whether these stem from poor retrieval, from the LLM's inability to process the retrieved content, or both"; models can use context when given "a minimally sufficient amount of relevant linguistic information". — [Court & Elsner](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.15625v3)

**Hus & Anastasopoulos (EMNLP 2024), "Back to School"**
- 16 languages (incl. Kalamang, Natugu, Gitksan, Guarani, Wolof, Kimbundu, Chokwe), GPT-4-turbo (128K), full grammar books 42,898-120,420 tokens, PanLex dictionaries, FLORES dev as sentence pool, LCS retrieval of 2 dictionary entries and 2 sentences per source word. Averages, chrF++: English→X baseline 19.2, W 22.7, W+S 30.3, W+S+G 26.7 (NLLB 38.0 on its 11 languages); X→English 34.1, 35.9, 35.7, 37.5 (NLLB 47.7). W+S was best for 12/16 languages into the language; adding the grammar book lowered the into-language average by 3.6. Kalamang eng→kgv: baseline 5.1, W 27.1, W+S 41.9, W+S+G 37.3; kgv→eng 11.3, 18.7, 27.6, 34.8. Authors suggest grammar books may matter only for languages with fewer than ~1,000 parallel sentences; OCR layout loss (tables, columns) as a limitation; cost ~$15,000. — [Hus & Anastasopoulos](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15263v1)

**Hus, Krasner, Anastasopoulos (AmericasNLP 2025), grammar materials for LLM post-correction**
- GPT-4o-mini with the full grammar book (44,698-165,311 tokens), dictionary, 2 retrieved sentences and a fine-tuned NLLB-3.3B suggestion; 13 Indigenous languages ↔ Spanish. Into the Indigenous language the LLM post-correction was usually worse than NLLB alone (chrF++ es→agr 16.67 vs 35.09; es→guc 9.12 vs 22.93; es→nah 15.98 vs 20.33; es→gn 29.95 vs 25.00 the exception); into Spanish it was slightly better (agr→es 36.59 vs 36.52; gn→es 33.84 vs 32.31; oto→es 18.24 vs 15.99; ctp→es 14.47 vs 26.16 the exception). Authors: "the LLM can use the grammar information to better understand the indigenous languages, but it is not enough to produce them, at least under the current prompt format and generation paradigm." — [Hus et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.americasnlp-1.10.pdf)

**Pei et al. (ACL 2025), Manchu**
- Manchu→English (Manchu→Chinese in an appendix); GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Llama3 1B-70B; dictionary (Norman 2020) + suffix list; 3,520 parallel pairs; grammars: Norman 1965 (short) and Gorelova 2002 (long); test 337. GPT-4o BLEU / chrF: zero-shot 3.44 / 21.86; + lexical entries 7.40 / 31.84; + suffix explanations 7.47 / 32.93; + collocations 7.55 / 32.71; + 10 BM25-retrieved examples 8.84 / 33.72 (random 10: 7.66 / 32.94). Then grammar on top: short book 8.26 / 33.12; long book 8.46 / 33.79; long book + its examples 8.90 / 33.77. Grammar without retrieved examples: 7.55 → 7.58 BLEU, 32.71 → 33.08 chrF. Chain-of-thought grammatical annotation lowered scores (8.01 / 33.13; with syntax 8.49 / 33.43). DeepSeek-V3 best setting 12.35 / 37.93. An enciphered-Manchu control (removing any pretraining knowledge) scored only slightly lower. Authors: "Grammars hardly help"; CoT errors in intermediate deductions propagate; "the model still cannot effectively use the grammar even with CoT". — [Pei et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11862v2)

**Shaw, Mee, Khouw, Leech, Wilson (2025), "Testing the Limits of Machine Translation from One Book" (Kanuri)**
- GPT-4.1, English→Kanuri only; Hutchison's 1981 grammar (373 pages, >300,000 tokens), 2,001 parallel sentences extracted from the grammar, a dictionary. Kalamang replication, chrF: zero-shot 17.40, grammar only 44.68, parallel only 56.65, grammar + parallel 58.17 (GPT-4.1 56.58 vs Gemini 1.5 Pro 59.00 on the MTOB setup). Kanuri dictionary-domain set: zero-shot 13.57, grammar 17.71, parallel 19.19, human linguist with the same materials 19.29; humanitarian set: 15.98 / 23.40 / 24.34. Native speakers preferred the LLM output's fluency over a native translation only 8.65% (grammar) / 15.38% (parallel) of the time, accuracy 21-35%. Authors: "a grammar is not a good approximation of a language when performing a translation task"; LLMs reach meaning more easily than grammaticality; chrF is too coarse for this. — [Shaw et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06665)

**C. Zhang et al. (ACL 2025), "Read it in two steps": code-augmented grammar rules (Zhuang, Kalamang)**
- ZhuangRules: 109 rules (word order 37, morphology 28, nominal categories 16, verbal 14, ...) with 608 paired sentences; Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct. Rule application given the gold rule, Zhuang→Chinese / Chinese→Zhuang (BLEU / chrF++): no rule 31.2 / 28.3 and 22.1 / 49.1; gold textual rule 51.4 / 50.5 and 51.8 / 78.7; gold rule rewritten as a code function 63.3 / 61.1 and 69.3 / 86.7; 2 parallel examples alone 65.6 / 61.3 and 63.4 / 80.1; gold text rule + 2 examples 70.7 / 68.0 and 80.7 / 91.0; gold code rule + 2 examples 73.4 / 71.4 and 81.2 / 91.6. A random (irrelevant) rule is at or below no rule. The test sentences are the short rule-illustrating pairs, which explains the high absolute chrF++. — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01796v1)
- Kalamang (97 rules and 152 examples extracted from the MTOB book, Qwen-72B, BLEU / chrF++): kgv→eng no rule 12.0 / 34.6, gold text rule 14.6 / 39.2, gold code rule 16.0 / 40.7; eng→kgv 39.9 / 63.8, 43.8 / 67.3, 44.5 / 67.0 (random rules ≈ no rule). Smaller gains than Zhuang, attributed to the book's "progressive" rules that reference other rules and to noisy regex extraction. — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01796v1)
- Retrieval is the bottleneck: asked to find the applicable rule in the whole book, Qwen-72B recall 52.8% (za2zh) / 49.4% (zh2za), Llama-70B 33.9 / 22.9, Qwen-7B 3.1 / 5.2; checking rule by rule with code rules 89.6 / 87.1. Full pipeline (chrF++ za2zh / zh2za): no retrieval 42.6 / 64.2; full-book retrieval + text application 43.1 / 66.5; rule-by-rule code retrieval + code application 55.7 / 74.3. Gains shrink with rule difficulty (hard rules, text rule + 2 ex.: 52.0 chrF++ vs easy 78.8). Authors: rule application "resembles procedural execution"; LLMs apply simple rules when explicitly provided and "struggle with more complex rules". — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01796v1); [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01796)

**2026 follow-ups**
- Reinforcement learning with a chrF reward (Qwen3-4B-Base, Llama-3.2-3B; context = ~20-34 retrieved dictionary entries, 3-5 retrieved pairs, 2 grammar passages ~2.8k tokens): En→Kalamang chrF (0-1 scale) base 0.2558, SFT 0.2860, RL 0.3464; ablating the RL model's context on En→Kal: full .3464, no grammar .3319, no sentences .2733, no dictionary .2626, instruction only .1700; Kal→En .3843 / .3821 / .2932 / .3146 / .1968. Grammar passages had the smallest effect (-1.5 and -0.2); the authors "suggest the model may be unable to extract useful information from grammar passages". — [RL paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06428v1)
- Pei et al. 2026, "Reasoning over Grammar" (Xibe, Chintang → English; Qwen3 4B-14B, Gemma 4): sentence-specific reasoning traces built from gold UD trees, a dictionary and a rule bank (77 / 82 rules), placed in context, gave up to +5.57 BLEU / +11.89 chrF (Gemma-4-E4B, Chintang); training the model to produce such traces gave smaller, inconsistent gains because generated analyses were wrong (structure, dependency relations, word senses). "LLMs can use grammatical information when given reliable analyses, but learning to generate those analyses remains a major bottleneck." — [Pei et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03782v2)
- Setiawan, Merx, Lau 2026, Dhao (the closest setting to ours: NT-trained, Tanakh-tested, English→Dhao): NLLB-600M fine-tuned on the NT's 7,644 verses scores 27.11 chrF++ on Genesis 1-500 (vs 36.17 in-domain); Gemini 2.5 Flash direct 0-shot 18.84, 5-shot 22.95; Gemini post-editing the NMT draft with 5 static examples 29.62; with ~137 retrieved NT verses (word-level retrieval, n=10) 35.28 chrF++ / 18.93 spBLEU; with the full 2,375-entry lexicon alone 31.32; combined 35.21 / 19.88. Adding the grammar's sentences to NMT fine-tuning lowered chrF++ (27.11 → 26.62). OOV rate rises from 8.1% (NT) to 25.9% (Tanakh). "Context volume drives performance": retrieval algorithms converge within ~0.5 chrF++. — [Setiawan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09982v1)

### Inferences
- The ranking is stable across 10 studies, 3 model generations and 20+ languages: dictionary/morph glosses and retrieved parallel sentences account for 85-100% of the gain; grammar prose adds 0-2 chrF on top (Pei: +0.05 chrF; Aycock: Book-all vs Book-para +3.6 eng→kgv but -0.3 kgv→eng at 5× tokens; Hus: -3.6 into the language; Shaw: +1.5 on Kalamang, within one SD), and into-the-language scores are always lower than out-of-the-language scores for the same material.
- Aycock's type-coverage correlation (r ≈ 0.92) is the most useful single number for us: in-context performance is a lexical-coverage curve. Our 41-44% of unseen verb forms are, by this measure, uncovered types that no amount of grammar prose has been shown to reconstruct.
- Grammar helps in exactly one form in the literature: a single, already-identified, sentence-relevant rule stated procedurally (code or step list) next to two examples of it (Zhang 2025), or a reliable sentence-specific gloss/analysis produced by an external tool (GrammaMT model-gloss, Pei 2026). Both require that something other than the LLM pick the rule or produce the analysis; when the LLM must find the rule in a book (52% recall for a 72B model, 3% for 7B) or write its own gloss (8% morpheme accuracy), the gain evaporates or turns negative.
- The AmericasNLP 2025 and Court & Elsner results both show the asymmetry our setting shows: grammar material raises comprehension (into Spanish) and does nothing or harm for production (into the language); the TAM-marking errors in Court & Elsner's output are the out-of-language shadow of our verb-form problem.

### Gaps
- LingoLLM's per-direction numbers (which of its 10 directions are into the endangered language and what they scored) could not be recovered from the html extraction; the paper does not discuss into-language failure at all.
- GrammaMT's reverse-direction (English→X) results exist only as a figure with no numbers.
- The Gemini 1.5 Table 55 "no book" row (55.5 chrF) is unverified and conflicts with every other zero-shot Kalamang result; the full report would need to be read at the table.
- No study was found that measures grammar material against a parallel corpus of our size (~8,000 verses): MTOB has ~500-3,300 pairs, Zhuang 4,944, Manchu 3,520, Kanuri 2,001; Hus & Anastasopoulos' guess that grammar matters only below ~1,000 pairs is the only statement on the size threshold and is a conjecture, not a measurement.

## Key question 2: Does grammar prose help once parallel examples and a dictionary are present? Do paradigm tables or interlinear glosses help with producing inflected forms the examples don't contain?

### Takeaway
No study found a reliable gain from grammar prose once parallel examples and a dictionary are in the prompt, and three found degradation; glosses help only out of the language and only when the gloss is accurate (produced by a trained glosser or gold data). No study tests paradigm tables for producing unseen inflected forms directly; the nearest evidence (conlang and synthetic-grammar studies) shows LLMs fail on multi-step inflection and on adding agreement the source lacks, and that fine-tuned skill does not transfer to typologically new features.

### Cited Findings
- Grammar on top of dictionary + retrieved examples: Manchu GPT-4o 8.84 / 33.72 → short grammar 8.26 / 33.12, long grammar 8.46 / 33.79 (BLEU / chrF); gains "marginal, not reflected in SBERT". — [Pei et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11862v2)
- Kalamang Gemini-1.5-Flash: explanations-only prompt (81.3k tokens) scores below the 9.0k-token wordlist (22.6 / 27.5 vs 29.1 / 27.9 chrF++); Book-all vs Book-para: +3.6 / -0.3 for 5.4× the tokens; adding the explanations to Guarani's examples lowered scores. — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- 16-language average, GPT-4-turbo: W+S 30.3 → W+S+G 26.7 chrF++ into the language; 35.7 → 37.5 out of it. — [Hus & Anastasopoulos](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15263v1)
- Kanuri/Kalamang GPT-4.1: parallel only 56.65 → grammar + parallel 58.17 chrF (SD ~3); grammar results "within one standard deviation in most cases". — [Shaw et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06665)
- Quechua→Spanish GPT-4o: every added context lowered BLEURT (0.66 → 0.53-0.59); for GPT-3.5 the morph glosses cut mistranslations 45 → 11 per 50 sentences but introduced 24 subject/TAM divergences and 14 fluency errors. — [Court & Elsner](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.15625v3)
- MTOB authors (original) claimed the ~100K-token grammar chunk gave a "qualitative leap" in eng→kgv grammaticality, but inconsistently and without a controlled number; Aycock's controlled split shows the book's examples, not its explanations, carry the effect. — [MTOB html](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16575v2); [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- Typological feature lists (a compact "binding rule list" of the Grambank kind) added nothing: Kalamang 10.8 / 13.9 vs 0-shot 11.0 / 12.7 chrF++; Typ + Book-para vs Book-para +0.6 / +0.5. — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- A single explicitly stated word-order rule was applied correctly in 1/10 cases; the same rule with per-sentence hints and chain-of-thought, 7/10. — [DiPMT++](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.19167v2)
- Interlinear glosses in the prompt (gloss-shot) and model-written glosses (chain-gloss) gave ±1 BLEU on endangered languages (3.94 → 3.41 / 4.25); accurate external glosses gave +12 BLEU (3.94 → 15.97); an oracle gloss +17.46 BLEU. Model-written glosses had 8.17% morpheme accuracy. — [GrammaMT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18702v2)
- Gloss *prediction* for Kalamang from the book: explanations-only prompt 21.0% morpheme accuracy vs examples-only 45.4% vs a fine-tuned ByT5 40.8% / GlossLM 43.8%; grammaticality judgement: examples-only beat explanations-only by up to 18 points on word-shuffle detection. — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- LingoLLM: a finite-state morphological analyzer (stems + features) gave +19% BLEURT on Gitksan, while the grammar's morphology chapters gave no significant gain for Arapaho once the analyzer supplied the features. — [LingoLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.18025v2)
- Suffix explanations added to the Manchu dictionary: 7.40 / 31.84 → 7.47 / 32.93 BLEU / chrF (Manchu→English). — [Pei et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11862v2)
- Conlang "explicit learning" (GPT-4o-mini; French/Latin ciphered; dictionary W, grammar excerpts G, incidental bitexts IB): English→conlang accuracy with W only 0.0, W+IB 1.8, W+G 5.9 (9.5 with CoT); conlang→English 1.8 / 14.4-22.8 / 31.2-32.4. Per phenomenon, baseline W+G CoT: simple partition 66%, masculine plural adjectives 31%, feminine plural 3% (needs masc.sg → fem.sg → plural, a two-step inflection), harder partitions 0-8%. After fine-tuning on 7 "courses" of CoT demonstrations: 62.9% average, but the typologically novel Latin partitions fell from 10.25% to 1.33%. "This ability diminishes as the complexity" of the phenomena increases. — [Marmonier, Bawden, Sagot 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09454v4)
- Synthetic-grammar transduction (Petty, Goe, Linzen 2026; GPT-5 family, Gemma 3): exact-match translation with the grammar in context falls from 0.967 at 57 rules to 0.912 at 837 and 0.487 at 8,037 rules (GPT-5); gpt-5-mini 0.975 → 0.013; Gemma-3 12B 0.000 throughout; translating from a language without verbal agreement into one with it is the hardest morphology condition; word-order differences had "negligible effects"; commonest errors: source-vocabulary leakage, recall of the wrong target word, omission. — [Petty et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07320v1)
- Rule application by difficulty (Qwen-72B, chrF++, Zhuang→Chinese): easy 65.6 (text rule) / 76.3 (code rule), medium 51.3 / 57.9, hard 34.6 / 48.6; rules with more required operations (add, reorder, select) are applied worse; morphology rules are 28 of 109. — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01796v1)
- Reliable sentence-specific analyses (gold UD trees + dictionary + triggered rules, in context) raised Chintang→English by up to +11.89 chrF; models trained to generate such analyses "copy the trace format but often get the analysis wrong". — [Pei et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03782v2)

### Inferences
- The form of grammar that works is per-sentence, pre-resolved and procedural: "for this sentence, rule X applies; here are its steps; here are two examples." Prose that the model must first retrieve and then instantiate does not; binding rule lists (typology) do not; per-item glosses the model writes itself do not.
- The direction asymmetry matters for paradigms: every gloss result is for comprehension (gloss → English). Producing an inflected form is the inverse problem (features → surface form), and the two studies that test it synthetically (Marmonier: feminine plural 3%; Petty: NoAgr→Agr hardest) show it failing precisely where more than one morphological step or an unseen agreement value is needed. For our 41-44% unseen verb forms the literature predicts: a paradigm table will not be applied reliably by the LLM alone; an external generator (analyzer/transducer, as LingoLLM's foma or GrammaMT's GlossLM) that produces the form, with the LLM selecting among candidates, is the only pattern with measured gains.
- "Grammar hints per clause gave no gain" in our runs matches Pei's CoT result (-0.6 chrF) and DiPMT++'s rule-only 1/10: the hint must already identify the slot and the value for the LLM to apply it, and even then the error rate on hard rules is high.

### Gaps
- No paper tests morphological paradigm tables (UniMorph-style) in the prompt for producing unseen inflected forms of a natural language; the nearest are the conlang and SCFG studies above, both synthetic.
- No paper reports what share of the target's inflected forms were absent from the context and how output accuracy varied with that share (our 41-44% measure has no published counterpart; Dhao reports only word-level OOV, 25.9%).
- The Aycock appendix past character 100,000 and Pei 2026's dictionary-only ablation (Appendix E) were not read; they may qualify which trace component carries the gain.

## Key question 3: Prompt length and attention: very long prompts, "lost in the middle", compact rule lists vs per-item annotations

### Takeaway
Long-context benchmarks show that use of material degrades with length even when it is present (11 of 13 models below 50% of their short-context score at 32K on NoLiMa), and the translation studies show the same in their own terms: performance tracks the fraction of relevant content, not the token count; a 99.6K-token book performs like its 18K-token parallel subset; full-book rule retrieval fails half the time at 72B; added context beyond ~140 retrieved verses or 1K tokens of monolingual text degrades output. Compact rule lists (typology) are not a cure: they are cheap but ineffective; per-sentence, pre-selected material is.

### Cited Findings
- Lost in the Middle (Liu et al. 2023): on multi-document QA and key-value retrieval, performance is highest when the relevant information is at the start or end and "degrades significantly" when it is in the middle; "current language models do not robustly make use of information in long input contexts". — [Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)
- NoLiMa (ICML 2025; 13 models claiming ≥128K): when the needle shares no literal words with the question, at 32K tokens 11 of 13 models fall below 50% of their short-context score; GPT-4o 99.3% → 69.7%; reasoning models and CoT do not fix it; attributed to the attention mechanism's difficulty retrieving without lexical matches. — [NoLiMa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167)
- Context Rot (Chroma 2025; 18 models incl. Claude Opus 4, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Qwen3): performance "consistently degrades" with input length on needle retrieval and on a trivial repeated-words task; a single distractor lowers it, four lower it more; low needle-question similarity degrades faster with length; a shuffled haystack beats a coherent one for all 18 models; LongMemEval: ~300-token focused prompts beat ~113K full prompts for every model. — [Context Rot](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)
- Translation-specific: chrF++ vs prompt tokens, no relationship (p = 0.997 / 0.78); vs type coverage, r = 0.92 / 0.93; Book-all 99.6K tokens ≈ Book-para 18.3K (34.4 / 34.4 vs 30.8 / 34.7); the authors invoke "lost-in-context" (RULER) and note Book-para's higher relevant-to-irrelevant ratio; Llama-3.1's 128K window could not hold the Kalamang or Guarani book at all. — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- Whole-book rule retrieval by the LLM: Qwen-72B 52.8% / 49.4% recall, Llama-70B 33.9 / 22.9, Qwen-7B 3.1 / 5.2 vs 89.6 / 87.1 checking rule by rule; irrelevant rules "sharply reduce translation quality"; full-book + code application exceeded the context limit. — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01796v1)
- More retrieved verses help up to a point and then hurt: Dhao word-level retrieval n=5 (~70 verses) 35.00 chrF++, n=10 (~137) 35.28, n=15 (~205) 35.16, n=20 (~272) 34.22 ("context noise"); sentence retrieval plateaus around K≈60; sentences + full lexicon together gave higher spBLEU but lower chrF++ than sentences alone ("syntactic noise from the larger context"). — [Setiawan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09982v1)
- Monolingual target text added to the prompt: +1K / +2K / +5K tokens gave 14.2 / 13.6 / 13.1 BLEU vs 13.3 without; a 14B model produced nothing usable with +1K; GPT-3.5 at +5K fabricated words. — [DiPMT++](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.19167v2)
- Grammar size in context (synthetic): GPT-5 0.967 → 0.750 → 0.487 exact match at 57 / 4,037 / 8,037 rules; gpt-5-mini collapses to 0.083 by 4,037 rules; sentence length 5.5 → 36.5 words: 0.950 → 0.734. Explanation: "maintaining and correctly applying a larger inventory of symbolic rules over longer derivations". — [Petty et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07320v1)
- MTOB's own baselines could fit only a 50K or 100K chunk of a 217K-token book, and the authors' grammar retrieval (512-token chunks, LCS) returned lexically similar passages rather than the relevant explanation. — [MTOB html](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16575v2)
- Compact rule lists: a Grambank/WALS-style typological prompt (68.4K tokens, Kalamang) gave 0-shot-level results; for Nepali and Guarani it lowered scores (Guarani eng→gug 26.6 → 21.1). — [Aycock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19151v2)
- Per-item annotation that works: 2-3 retrieved examples + ~20-34 dictionary entries + 2 grammar passages (~2.8K tokens) is the whole context of the 2026 RL study; the Manchu best prompt is dictionary entries for the sentence's words + 10 BM25 examples; GrammaMT's gain comes from one gloss line per sentence. — [RL paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06428v1); [Pei et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11862v2); [GrammaMT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18702v2)

### Inferences
- Our ~1 MB of prompt material (~250K+ tokens) sits in the regime where every benchmark shows degraded use: Aycock's book (99.6K) already bought nothing per token, Zhang's 72B model missed half the applicable rules in a far smaller rule set, and Chroma/NoLiMa show the loss is not confined to the middle but grows with length and with distractors. The literature's working configurations are 1-30K tokens, selected per sentence.
- The evidence does not support "compact binding rule list" as the alternative; it supports "per-sentence selection by a tool": the 5*-shot retrieved examples at 0.8K tokens (38.9 chrF++ eng→kgv) outperformed the full 99.6K book (34.4) in Aycock.
- A practical reading of the Dhao curve for our 8,000-verse corpus: ~60-140 retrieved verses per source verse is the measured sweet spot for English→X post-editing with a 2025 model; beyond ~200 it costs chrF.

### Gaps
- No translation study varies the position of the grammar or examples within the prompt, so there is no direct "lost in the middle" number for translation material; the position evidence is from QA/retrieval benchmarks only.
- No study measures the degradation for prompts in the 250K-1M token range with Gemini 2.5 / Claude 4 / GPT-5-class models on an unseen language; the largest translation prompts measured are ~113K (Aycock) and ~165K (AmericasNLP 2025).

## Key question 4: Discourse particles, connectives, clause chaining in LLM translation into Papuan or Bantu languages

### Takeaway
I found no study that evaluates LLM translation of clause chaining, switch-reference, narrative tense markers or discourse particles in a Papuan or Bantu language; the only Papuan-language evidence is Kalamang (MTOB and its follow-ups, sentence-level, no discourse analysis), and the only Bantu numbers are GrammaMT's Swahili→English and an agreement-probing study on multilingual encoders.

### Cited Findings
- Kalamang is the Papuan language in the literature; the MTOB human baseline had studied "many languages, though none Austronesian or Papuan". All Kalamang evaluation is on 50-100 isolated sentences with chrF; no discourse-level analysis is reported. — [MTOB html](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16575v2)
- GrammaMT, Swahili→English (Llama-3 70B, GlossLM data): few-shot 22.41 BLEU, gloss-shot 22.18, chain-gloss 23.53; Yoruba (non-Bantu) 11.98 → 16.32 with gloss-shot. No into-Swahili numbers. — [GrammaMT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18702v2)
- A controlled syntactic-knowledge evaluation of multilingual (encoder) models on Basque, Hindi and Swahili reports that Swahili noun-class agreement was the hardest phenomenon, with models "often performing near random guessing"; this is from a search snippet, not read in full, and does not concern generative LLM translation. — [Controlled Evaluation of Syntactic Knowledge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.07474)
- The translation studies that analysed errors into a morphologically rich language found the grammatical errors concentrated in TAM and subject marking (Quechua; "TAM divergences were far more frequent than subject divergences"), and native speakers preferring the LLM's fluency only 7-15% of the time (Kanuri). — [Court & Elsner](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.15625v3); [Shaw et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06665)
- The synthetic-grammar study found that mapping from a source without agreement into a target with it is the hardest morphology condition, and that the model credits only exact forms. — [Petty et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07320v1)
- The nearest discourse-particle benchmark is MalayPrag (2026, colloquial Malay): "substantial challenges for current LLMs in accurately connecting discourse particles with their pragmatic functions"; Malay, not Bantu or Papuan, and comprehension, not translation. — [MalayPrag](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.28782)
- Bantu clause chains are "commonly associated with the narrative marker ka-" and can span sentences; Papuan chains combine switch-reference-marked medial clauses ending in a final independent verb (linguistic background only, no MT work). — [Clause Chaining in the Languages of the World](https://academic.oup.com/book/59261/chapter/499385188); [Clause linkage in Papuan languages](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/slcs.121.02fol/html)

### Inferences
- The question is open in the literature. What exists points the same way as the rest: medial-verb morphology and narrative-tense markers are inflectional choices made per clause on discourse grounds, which is the class of thing (TAM, agreement, multi-step inflection) that the measured studies show LLMs getting wrong even with grammar in context; our clause-level hints matching Pei's negative CoT result is consistent with that.
- Zinza's ka- remote past / narrative question (our corpus test found it weak, 2.2 [1.0-4.8]) is a corpus-frequency question first; nothing published suggests a grammar description would settle what the corpus does not show.

### Gaps
- No LLM-MT evaluation of switch-reference, medial verbs or clause chaining was found for any language (searches: "switch-reference clause chaining machine translation LLM", "Papuan LLM translation", "Bantu noun class agreement LLM translation"); nor any into-Bantu LLM evaluation with grammatical error categories.
- No Bible-domain study besides Dhao (English→Dhao, Austronesian) was found that combines in-context verse retrieval with chrF into an unseen language; Dhao does not analyse verb forms.
