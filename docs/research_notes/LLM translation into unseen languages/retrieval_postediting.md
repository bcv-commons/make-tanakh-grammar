# Retrieval of examples and post-editing strategies for LLM translation into low-resource languages (2023-2026)

Scope: example selection for in-context MT, number of examples vs prompt length, LLM post-editing of NMT (NLLB-class) output, reference-free selection among candidates (MBR, QE), and lexical copying vs grammatical form. Numbers are given with model, language and data size where the source states them. Our setting for reference: Papuan (Kuni) and Bantu (Zinza) targets, ~8,000-verse NT corpus; LLM agent with a 1 MB retrieval pack at chrF ~62-70; fine-tuned NLLB-600M at chrF 70 with worse grammar (affix F1 40 vs 50) and dropped negations; verse-level word retrieval covers 24% of clauses, clause-structure retrieval 66%.

## Q1. Which example-selection methods give the largest gains for low-resource targets, and does source-side structural retrieval beat lexical similarity?

### Takeaway
For targets the model barely knows, retrieving examples by similarity to the source (BM25, LCS, or multilingual sentence embeddings such as SONAR) beats random examples by a wide margin, and the margin grows with the model's weakness in the language; the published structure-aware methods (dependency-tree coverage, POS sequences) add at most a few tenths of a COMET point over word-based coverage on high-resource pairs, and structure alone (POS sequence) was worse than random in the one unseen-language test. Nobody has yet tested retrieval by clause type, tense or negation.

### Cited Findings

French and German gains are 0.1-0.5 laCOMET for most LLMs and narrow as shots increase. SONAR was best overall, BM25 second; R-BM25 re-ranking did not improve BM25; the pool was FLORES-200 dev (997 pairs). Retrieval mainly helps the rate of correct-language output for Wolof. — [arXiv 2408.00397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00397), [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.68/)

- DiPMT++ (ACL Findings 2024), Zhuang (unseen) with a dictionary and ~5K parallel sentences, Qwen-14B-chat, 3-shot, BLEU/chrF zh→za: random exemplars 9.2/40.0, POS-sequence retrieval 7.8/30.1, BM25 12.6/41.7; za→zh: random 14.7/14.1, POS-sequence 12.1/12.8, BM25 19.5/17.8. - Many-shot low-resource study (2026; 10 languages incl. Efik, Ibibio, Emakhuwa, Tamazight, Quechua; Gemini 2.5 Flash, eng→X, chrF++, 10-language averages): BM25 vs random at 5 shots 36.7 vs 34.4 (+2.4), 50 shots 40.3 vs 37.5 (+2.7), 250 shots 41.2 vs 40.2 (+1.1), 1,000 shots 41.8 vs 41.7 (+0.1); dense retrieval (Qwen3-Embedding-8B) gave negligible differences from BM25 in the ordering experiments. — [arXiv 2604.02596](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02596)
- In-context example generation (ACL Findings 2025) notes that retrieval approaches assume "a large pool of human-annotated pairs, which may not hold for low-resource languages" and proposes generating demonstrations instead. — [arXiv 2506.00507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00507)

### Inferences
- The gain from similarity retrieval is inversely related to the model's prior knowledge of the target: Wolof +8-12 laCOMET at 10 shots, Swahili on Gemma 7B +0.9, French/German +0.1-0.5. Kuni and Zinza are on the Wolof end, so retrieval quality should matter more for us than for most published settings.
- The two unseen-language tests that compared retrieval keys (DiPMT++ POS sequence vs BM25; the RL paper's LCS vs embeddings) both favoured lexical/string matching over the structural or semantic alternative. The only positive structural result (SCOI) is a few tenths of a COMET point on high-resource pairs and depends on a parser. Our 66% vs 24% coverage figure measures retrievability, not translation gain; the literature predicts that structure retrieval pays only when combined with lexical matching, and that its value rests on structure quality. Our source-side structure (MACULA/BHSA clause layouts, gold rather than parsed) removes SCOI's main failure cause, so the case for testing it is better than the published numbers suggest, but it is untested.
- Word-level retrieval (one query per source word, Dhao) resembles our clause-package design more than sentence-level BM25 does, and it was the best method there, chiefly through volume (k≈137 verses).
- Coverage re-ranking (R-BM25) is not a reliable gain: two independent replications found plain BM25 at least as good.
- Grammar prose is a poor retrieval target: in Court and Elsner and in Aycock et al. it added nothing or hurt; the exception is a rule stated for a specific decision (DiPMT++'s modifier-order rule with chain-of-thought fixed 7/10 cases), which is more like a store entry than a grammar chapter.

### Gaps
- No paper retrieves by tense, polarity, clause type or speech-act; the nearest are POS-sequence retrieval (worse than random on Zhuang) and dependency coverage (high-resource only). Whether clause-structure retrieval beats word retrieval for an 8,000-verse corpus is unmeasured.
- Zebaze et al. test k ≤ 20 and only pools of ~1,000; no published retrieval study uses a pool the size of a NT with verse-level alignment to the source text as the retrieval key.

## Q2. How many examples: gains vs prompt length, and where does it saturate (k=5, 10, 30, 100, 1,000)?

### Takeaway
For low-resource targets quality rises roughly log-linearly with examples and keeps rising to about 250 retrieved or 1,000 random examples (85k+ tokens), but retrieval compresses the curve: 50 retrieved ≈ 250 random, and the retrieval advantage vanishes by 1,000 shots. Out-of-domain examples (Bible text on FLORES) plateau or degrade with more shots; 5 well-retrieved examples beat a whole 100k-token grammar book.

### Cited Findings
- Many-shot ICL (Agarwal et al. 2024), Gemini 1.5 Pro, eng→Bemba and eng→Kurdish, chrF2++ on 150 sentences: small gains from 1 to 10 shots; the full 997-pair dev set (~85K tokens) gives +15.3% (Bemba) and +4.5% (Kurdish) relative to 1-shot; "performance improves monotonically"; the paper cites NLLB at 35 chrF++ (Bemba) and Google at 40 (Kurdish) as references that many-shot beats. — [arXiv 2404.11018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.11018)
- Many-shot low-resource study (2026), k ∈ {0,1,5,10,25,50,100,250,500,1,000}, Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4.1, Llama 3.3 70B, Gemma 3 27B: random-shot average chrF++ (eng→X, 10 languages) 34.4 (5) → 37.5 (50) → 40.2 (250) → 41.7 (1,000); BM25 36.7 → 40.3 → 41.2 → 41.8. Gains flatten past ~250 shots (250→1,000: +1.5 random, +0.6 BM25); for some languages 250 or 500 beat 1,000, especially X→eng. 0-shot → 1,000-shot gains range +3.7 to +35.6 chrF++ by language. Bible-domain examples plateau or degrade with more shots on FLORES; Efik and Ibibio benefit more from religious-domain data than other languages. Length-based ordering has no significant effect. Open-weight models ran out of context at high k. The full experiment cost over $30,000 in API credits. — [arXiv 2604.02596](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02596)
- Zebaze et al.: for Swahili and Wolof the gap to random keeps growing to 20 shots with no plateau, except OLMo 7B whose 2,048-token context causes empty outputs at 20 shots; low-resource directions "would require more shots, typically between 2 and 5" just to get correct-language output. — [arXiv 2408.00397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00397)
- Aycock et al.: 5\*-shot (0.8k tokens) 38.9 chrF++ eng→kgv vs the full book (99.6k tokens) 34.4 and all book sentences unretrieved (15.6k) 26.6. — [arXiv 2409.19151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19151)
- Dhao: sentence-level retrieval plateaus around k≈60; word-level retrieval still improves at effective k≈137; lexicon performance rises steadily with the number of entries given. — [arXiv 2601.09982](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09982)
- DiPMT++: adding raw monolingual Zhuang text to the prompt (GPT-3.5-Turbo, zh→za BLEU) baseline 13.3 → 1K tokens 14.2 → 2K 13.6 → 5K 13.1; at 5K the model fabricated words. — [arXiv 2402.19167](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19167)
- Moslem et al.: fuzzy 2 → 5 → 10 shots, Kinyarwanda chrF++ 36.66 → 39.84 → 41.44. — [arXiv 2301.13294](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13294)
- Agrawal et al.: concatenating several random examples reduces the damage from a noisy example. — [arXiv 2212.02437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.02437)
- Many-shot vs fine-tuning: Aycock et al. fine-tuned NLLB-1.3B on ~1.2k book sentences in one GPU-hour to 34.2 chrF++ eng→kgv vs Gemini with the same sentences in context at 26.6 (but 28.6 vs 33.1 kgv→eng). — [arXiv 2409.19151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19151)

### Inferences
- With retrieval, most of the gain is in by 50-100 examples and nearly all by 250; past that, more context mainly buys robustness, and in-domain relevance matters more than count. For a 1 MB pack (~250k tokens) the evidence says the pack's size is not the limiting factor; its relevance per clause is.
- Unretrieved bulk (whole corpus, grammar prose, monolingual text) is worse than a small retrieved set, and can introduce fabricated words (DiPMT++ at 5K tokens).
- The log-linear curve means that doubling retrieved examples from 50 to 100 yields on the order of +1 chrF++; the same budget spent on better per-clause matches (word-level or clause-level retrieval) gave +4-5 chrF++ in Dhao.

### Gaps
- No study plots gains for k between 20 and 250 with retrieval on a Bible-domain test set; the 2026 study's Bible examples were out-of-domain for FLORES.
- No study reports the trade-off between prompt length and latency/cost at the chapter scale (many verses sharing one pack).

## Q3. Does LLM post-editing of NLLB-class output help for languages the LLM cannot write? Which errors does it fix and which does it introduce?

### Takeaway
Yes, when the post-editor is given retrieved examples and a lexicon: in the closest analogue to our setting (Dhao, NT-trained NLLB-600M, Gemini 2.5 Flash) the pipeline rose from 27.1 to 35.2 chrF++ on out-of-domain Genesis while the LLM alone scored 23 and post-editing without retrieval 28-30; across FLORES low-resource pairs the gain over NLLB-54B averaged +2.8 chrF++ with one language losing 1.3. The post-editor fixes repetition loops, hallucinated names and idioms, and in-domain terminology; it introduces wrong lexical substitutions from glossary entries and, in the Indigenous-language direction, fails outright when the LLM cannot produce the language.

### Cited Findings
- Dhao (NLLB-600M fine-tuned on the NT; Gemini 2.5 Flash), chrF++ on Genesis 1-500: NMT alone 27.11 (in-domain NT reference 36.17); NMT + grammar 26.62; LLM direct 0-/5-shot 18.84/22.95; LLM post-edit of the NMT draft, no retrieval, 0-/5-shot 27.94/29.62; post-edit + full dictionary 31.32; post-edit + word-level retrieved sentences 35.28; final combined 35.21 (spBLEU 19.88 vs 7.66 for NMT alone). Errors fixed: NMT repetition loops (GEN 10:17) detected and re-translated; NMT hallucinated "King David" in GEN 11:5, corrected to Lamatua from the source and lexicon; "became the father of" (GEN 11:17) rendered correctly from retrieved examples. — [arXiv 2601.09982](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09982)
- TULUN (2025; Gemini 2.0 Flash post-editor with BM25-retrieved translation-memory matches and n-gram-matched glossary entries), chrF++. Tetun (glossary 2,698, TM 1,018 sentences) / Bislama (dictionary 5,769, TM 3,353): MADLAD-400-10B alone 35.78/15.22; Gemini 10-shot alone 44.59/45.37; MADLAD + Gemini 47.87/47.94. FLORES-200 (NLLB-54B alone → NLLB + Gemini): Tok Pisin 41.61 → 46.80, Dzongkha 34.67 → 35.76, Quechua 26.87 → 32.40, Rundi 42.51 → 41.17, Lingala 47.99 → 50.58, Assamese 35.91 → 39.80; average 38.26 → 41.09 (+2.83); Gemini 10-shot alone 38.94. For Rundi the LLM made wrong word changes from glossary entries; without the glossary +0.25 instead of −1.34. No human quality evaluation (usability survey of two co-authors only). — [arXiv 2505.18683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18683)
- Moslem et al., English→Kinyarwanda: NLLB-3.3B alone 25.17 spBLEU / 52.59 chrF++; GPT-3 with 5 fuzzy matches plus the NLLB output of the segment 25.59/53.12; plus NLLB output of the fuzzy matches 27.52/53.23. The LLM alone with 10 fuzzy matches: 17.87/41.44. For French, adding OPUS output lowered GPT-3 (51.94 → 47.95 spBLEU). — [arXiv 2301.13294](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13294)
- GMU at AmericasNLP 2025: an LLM prompted with grammar reference materials corrects a fine-tuned encoder-decoder system's output; improvements only when translating from the Indigenous languages into Spanish; into the Indigenous languages the LLM could not produce the language with that prompt format and NLLB did better. — [ACL Anthology 2025.americasnlp-1.10](https://aclanthology.org/2025.americasnlp-1.10/)
- Mufu (ICLR 2025): LLMs fine-tuned to correct several auxiliary candidate translations in the prompt are robust to poor candidates and beat NLLB-1.3B distilled in 64% of low- and very-low-resource eng→X FLORES pairs; distilled models keep an average +3.1 chrF over a fine-tune-only baseline. — [arXiv 2409.13949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13949)
- Raunak et al. (EMNLP Findings 2023): GPT-4 post-editing improved the state of the art on WMT-22 en-zh, en-de, zh-en, de-en; "GPT-4 could produce hallucinated edits"; human raters found a large gain in edit trustworthiness over the previous LLM. — [arXiv 2305.14878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14878)
- Ki and Carpuat (NAACL Findings 2024), LLaMA-2 post-editing zh-en, en-de, en-ru with MQM feedback: prompting improves TER, BLEU and COMET; fine-grained error annotations help only after fine-tuning. — [arXiv 2404.07851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07851)
- Sinhala APE diagnostic (2026; also Marathi, Tamil): a black-box test varying an edit-distance penalty finds "Binary Collapse" (the post-editor copies the MT or makes off-target edits) and "Confident Miscalibration" (confidence signals do not separate useful from unnecessary edits). A secondary summary of the paper reports that an NLLB-600M post-editor over-edits indiscriminately (copy rates: accuracy 52.6%, style 50.6%, fluency 34.1%). — [arXiv 2609.29680](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29680) (copy rates are from a search summary of the paper, not verified in the text)
- eBible corpus (2023): NLLB-600M fine-tuned on Scripture reaches roughly 43-58 chrF3 on NT completion depending on family and setup; tokenizer gaps for some scripts reduce accuracy. — [arXiv 2304.09919](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09919)

### Inferences
- The pattern across Dhao, TULUN and Moslem is the same: NMT alone < LLM post-editing without resources ≲ LLM alone with retrieval < NMT draft + LLM with retrieval. The draft contributes the target wording the LLM cannot generate; the retrieval pack contributes the evidence to check it. Without the pack (Dhao +0.8 to +2.5; Moslem +0.5 chrF++) the gain is marginal.
- The NMT+LLM gain is largest where the NMT is weakest (Dhao out-of-domain, Tetun) and can be negative where the NMT is already good and the glossary is noisy (Rundi). With our NLLB at chrF 70, the expected gain is small on chrF but concentrated on the error classes NLLB is known for: dropped negations, names, repetition; those are the cases the Dhao paper documents as fixed.
- The direction matters: every failure report is into the low-resource language; the LLM must be able to produce target wording, which it gets from the draft plus examples, not from grammar prose (GMU).
- Introduced errors come from over-editing and from unreliable lexical resources; a post-editor should be constrained to edit only with evidence (a retrieved example or a store entry), and glossary use should be checked, not assumed.

### Gaps
- No study measures negation, names or agreement separately before and after post-editing; the Dhao evidence is three verse-level examples. Our own affix-F1/negation checks would be the first such measurement.
- The GMU numbers (into-Indigenous losses) and the Sinhala copy rates could not be read from the PDFs here.
- No SIL/Serval publication with chrF for Scripture Forge drafts or for LLM post-editing of them was found; only a workshop deck stating that drafts "require editing by skilled translators" ([deck](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1t2R8e5dpjoign9IkKMaFI-T_Wk6NpYZ0/htmlpresent)).

## Q4. Selecting among candidates without a reference: MBR with chrF/COMET, self-consistency, QE; typical gains and cost

### Takeaway
MBR over 5-50 samples reliably improves LLM translation, with the largest gains for weak models and into-X directions (+7 to +13 COMET for LLaMA-7B into DE/RU/CS/UK with 50 samples; +0.8 to +2.7 for ChatGPT), and beats QE reranking; cost is ~3× greedy at N=5 and 19-27× at N=50. For an unseen target, learned QE (CometKiwi) is unvalidated and chrF-MBR inflates chrF evaluation, so MBR gains must be judged by a different metric or by humans.

### Cited Findings
- Farinhas, de Souza and Martins (EMNLP 2023), WMT22 EN↔DE/RU/CS/UK, MBR utility COMET-22, ranking by CometKiwi: ChatGPT COMET gains over greedy at N=5/20/50: EN-DE +0.76/+1.08/+1.24, EN-RU +1.11/+1.64/+1.87, EN-CS +1.33/+1.93/+2.17, EN-UK +1.57/+2.37/+2.65; X-EN +0.28 to +0.66. LLaMA 7B with 50 samples: EN-DE +6.7, EN-RU +12.2, EN-CS +11.3, EN-UK +13.0; Alpaca 7B +7.9 to +13.4; X-EN +1.7 to +3.1. MBR beats QE reranking on COMET in nearly all settings. Instruction-based selection (choose/generate the best from the hypotheses in the prompt) falls short of greedy and costs 6-7×. Token cost of MBR ~3× greedy at N=5, 8-11× at N=20, 19-27× at N=50. Self-consistency is MBR with an exact-match utility. — [arXiv 2310.11430](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11430); self-consistency framing in [arXiv 2407.15343](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2407.15343)
- WMT 2024 "ensembles of MBR utilities": prior work finds MBR improves automatic metrics on high-, medium- and low-resource pairs, and human raters prefer MBR/QE outputs over greedy (Freitag et al. 2022, 2023; Tomani et al. 2023). — [ACL Anthology 2024.wmt-1.109](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wmt-1.109.pdf)
- Metric interference (2025): with pools of 20 epsilon-sampled candidates and MBR under COMET-22, BLEURT, chrF or BLEU, evaluating with the same metric used as utility inflates scores. — [arXiv 2503.08327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08327)
- Chasing COMET (2024): COMET-MBR self-improvement gives significant gains for all pairs tested including cs-uk and en-Hausa. — [arXiv 2405.11937](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11937)
- Source-based MBR (ACL 2025): QE reranking is a form of MBR; using source-side support hypotheses with a QE utility outperforms both QE reranking and standard MBR under XCOMET/MetricX, including low-resource sub-setups. — [arXiv 2406.11632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11632)
- Yang et al. (NAACL 2024): MBR "can significantly improve" multilingual LLM translation but is expensive; DPO on MBR preferences internalises the gain. — [ACL Anthology 2024.naacl-short.34](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-short.34)
- JHARNA-MT (MMLoSo 2025; Bhili, Gondi, Mundari, Santali ↔ Hindi/English): candidates from retrieval, lexical SMT and a LoRA-tuned NLLB-200 combined by MBR with a BLEU+chrF utility; 2nd on the private leaderboard. — [ACL Anthology 2025.mmloso-1.13](https://aclanthology.org/2025.mmloso-1.13/)
- Typologically informed reranking (2026; GPT-5.2 beam width 32, top-4 rescored with a rule-based typology score, nine targets incl. Sinhala, Tamil, Swahili): automatic-metric deltas vs baseline mostly small or negative (Sinhala BLEU −3.20, Tamil −2.20, Arabic +0.19); the authors claim gains in expert review. — [arXiv 2602.01162](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01162)
- CometKiwi (WMT22) is InfoXLM fine-tuned on WMT17-20 DA and MLQE-PE; the WMT23 systems were "developed to achieve good multilingual generalization and to accommodate previously unseen languages". — [WMT22 QE paper](https://aclweb.org/anthology/2022.wmt-1.60.pdf), [arXiv 2309.11925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11925)
- A 2025 synthetic-data study notes "CometKiwi has not been explicitly trained on any of the languages in our dataset, even though its underlying model, XLM-R, includes them." — [arXiv 2505.14423](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14423)
- SCFG-transduction study (2026): BLEU and chrF++ consistently overestimate exact-match translation accuracy, and the gap widens as tasks get harder. — [arXiv 2604.07320](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07320)

### Inferences
- The Farinhas pattern (weak model into X gains 10× more than a strong model) predicts that MBR over an LLM's Kuni/Zinza samples would gain several chrF points, more than on any high-resource pair; but the utility must be chrF (no neural metric covers these languages), so the measured chrF gain will be partly metric interference. Validate on chrF++ against the held-out human chapters with a different utility (e.g., chrF for selection, BLEU/token-F1 and the affix F1 and negation checks for evaluation), or by human judgement.
- CometKiwi should be treated as unvalidated for Kuni (a Papuan language outside XLM-R's 100 languages) and weakly grounded for Zinza; the published "unseen language" generalisation claim is about pairs absent from the QE training data, not languages absent from the encoder.
- A consensus pool mixing systems (LLM samples + NLLB + retrieval candidates, as in JHARNA-MT) is the published way to get complementary strengths without a reference; our LLM draft and NLLB draft are two such candidates, and chrF-MBR between them plus a few LLM samples is cheap (N≈5 is 3× greedy).
- Rule-based typological reranking shows that structure-aware selection does not show up in chrF/BLEU; if we rerank by affix or negation checks, expect the gain to appear in those checks, not in chrF.

### Gaps
- No published MBR gains in chrF for an unseen target with an LLM; all low-resource MBR numbers found are under COMET-family metrics.
- No validation of CometKiwi (or any QE) against human judgements on a language outside XLM-R's coverage.

## Q5. Do LLMs copy wording from examples but inflect poorly? Methods that improved inflection

### Takeaway
The evidence consistently shows that gains come from parallel examples and dictionaries (lexical material), not from grammatical descriptions, and that targets with agreement morphology are the hardest; LLMs underperform purpose-built systems on nonce-word inflection. What helped grammatical form: morphological segmentation plus dictionary entries in the prompt (Quechua, LingoLLM), a specific rule applied with chain-of-thought at the decision point (7/10 word-order cases), typological hints for glossing (Gram F1 +1.5), and letting the model state inflection rules before applying them (matching supervised inflection systems with 10 shots).

### Cited Findings
- Aycock et al.: "almost all improvements stem from the book's parallel examples rather than its grammatical explanations"; explanations only 22.6 chrF++ eng→kgv vs parallel sentences 30.8 vs wordlist alone 29.1; on Kalamang interlinear glossing, typology + parallel sentences reach Gram F1 50.5 vs 49.0 without typology, 45.2 for 10-shot and 15.6 for explanations only; a supervised GlossLM reaches 50.4. Grammaticality judgement: parallel sentences beat explanations by up to 18 points on the shuffle test. Human evaluation of grammar vs lexical errors: none (seven qualitative examples). — [arXiv 2409.19151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19151)
- MTOB (Tanzer et al. 2023): best LLM 44.7 chrF kgv→eng, 45.8 eng→kgv vs a human learner 51.6 / 57.0 from the same book. — [arXiv 2309.16575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16575)
- Court and Elsner: morphological segmentation + dictionary context helps most (GPT-3.5 BLEURT 0.19 → 0.44, Llama 3 0.15 → 0.39); grammar lessons and corpus examples null or negative; even GPT-4o's best condition had grammatical divergence (subject/TAM errors) in over 20% of outputs; GPT-3.5 produced 30/50 complete mistranslations zero-shot, sharply reduced by the morph context. — [arXiv 2406.15625](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15625)
- LingoLLM (ACL Findings 2024): a morphological analyser, dictionary and grammar description in the prompt raised GPT-4 from 0 to 10.5 BLEU averaged over 10 directions in 8 endangered languages. — [arXiv 2402.18025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18025)
- DiPMT++: stating the modifier-order rule fixed 1/10 cases; chain-of-thought with the modifiers hinted fixed 7/10; fuzzy matching helps with compounds; no quantification of morphological errors. — [arXiv 2402.19167](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19167)
- RL on unseen-language prompts: dictionary entries matter most (removing them 0.346 → 0.263), parallel sentences next (0.273), grammar least (0.332); SFT drops "cold" and renders "bathe" as "wash his hands" in the case study; no morphology analysis. — [arXiv 2606.06428](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06428)
- SCFG study (2026; gpt-5 family, Gemma 3): with only a grammar in the prompt, exact-match falls from 0.967 at 57 rules to 0.487 at 8,037 rules; source/target word-order differences had negligible effect; agreement type matters most, with translation from a language without verb agreement into one with agreement the hardest condition; unfamiliar scripts hurt (pointed Hebrew 0%); frequent errors are recalling the wrong target word, source-vocabulary leakage and omissions. — [arXiv 2604.07320](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07320)
- Counting the bugs in ChatGPT's wugs (EMNLP 2023; English, German, Tamil, Turkish nonce-word inflection): ChatGPT "massively underperforms purpose-built systems, particularly in English". — [ACL Anthology 2023.emnlp-main.401](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.401/)
- Subword segmentation in LLMs (EMNLP 2024): for morphologically complex languages, many inflected forms have insufficient coverage in training data. — [ACL Anthology 2024.emnlp-main.672](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.672.pdf)
- Guiding LLMs with their own linguistic knowledge (2025): a two-stage prompt in which the model first writes the target language's inflection rules, then applies them, combined with 10-shot Gemma 3 27B, matches supervised inflection baselines trained on much larger data; the benefit shrinks as in-context examples increase. — [Vetulani et al. 2025, AMU Press](https://press.amu.edu.pl/pub/media/productattach/1/9/19_vetulani_human_language_2025_amup_oa.pdf)
- Typological reranking: systematic errors in low-resource LLM output include missing morphological markers, wrong word order and a frequent-but-wrong word chosen over the contextually correct one. — [arXiv 2602.01162](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01162)
- Related-language pivots (2026): few-shot pivoting through a linguistically related language gives modest, configuration-sensitive gains when the target is poorly represented, diminishing for better-represented varieties. — [arXiv 2603.16660](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16660)
- Zebaze et al.: retrieval chiefly raises the rate of correct-language output for Wolof; scores stay low even once the language is right. — [arXiv 2408.00397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00397)

### Inferences
- The literature's "parallel examples beat grammar" result is a result about grammar prose; the interventions that improved form are all local and decision-shaped: a segmented word with its gloss, a rule applied at a marked decision, a paradigm the model wrote out itself. That matches our store (affix entries, cells, claims) better than a grammar wiki page does, and suggests packs should carry the inflected forms needed for the verse rather than the rule.
- Agreement is the known hard case (SCFG study; Court and Elsner's subject/TAM errors), which is where Zinza's class concords and Kuni's actor/undergoer agreement sit; our affix-F1 gap (LLM 50 vs NLLB 40) is already better than the NMT, and the published fix for the remaining gap is morphological segmentation in the prompt and verifying agreement after generation, not more examples (Vetulani: the rule's benefit shrinks with more shots).
- chrF overstates grammatical correctness (SCFG study), so an LLM that copies lexical wording from retrieved verses will score well on chrF while mis-inflecting; the affix F1 and negation checks are the right measures, and the chrF 62-70 vs 70 comparison with NLLB should not be read as a grammar comparison.
- No paper quantifies "copying from examples" as such; the nearest evidence is that wrong-vocabulary recall and source leakage dominate the error profile, and that retrieval gains come mostly from correct-language and lexical coverage. The claim that LLMs copy well but inflect poorly is supported indirectly, not measured.

### Gaps
- No study separates lexical accuracy from inflectional accuracy for LLM translation into an unseen language; glossing Gram F1 (Aycock) is the closest proxy.
- No published test of paradigm tables in the prompt for translation (as opposed to inflection tasks); Vetulani's result is on inflection benchmarks.
- No study on negation specifically; the dropped-negation problem is reported only in our own checks.
